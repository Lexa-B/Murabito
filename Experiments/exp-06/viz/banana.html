Step 1: the world on the banana

The banana is a slot bent along a circular arc, sized like Honshu at its Kantō width: arc A = 80°, centreline radius R = 237 ri (about 1,300 km along the axis), width w = 60 ri (about 236 km, coast to coast across Kantō). It is turned so the top tip of the centreline heads due north (90°), which puts the outer apex facing ESE, the Pacific side. The outline is walked anticlockwise from the outer apex, and θ is the fraction of its length walked, in degrees. The world, 36 ri flat to flat and north-up, is centred on the outline point at θ moved inland by d. Units are ri.

The edge reading: an edge the outline crosses is a shore edge; an edge it does not cross is sea or land by where its midpoint lies. The rule from the napkin, no more than four of either, is checked live. The two shore crossings are where the surface of step 2 passes through sea level on the world's edges.

Step 2: tectonics, as two curves

Height anywhere, above or below the sea, is h = H(s) · F(a). F(a) is one cross-section, the same everywhere: a counts half-widths from the centreline, the coasts at ±1, and the table runs from a deep basin three half-widths inside to a trench three half-widths out. From the outer coast inward: a wide plain, the spine a little past the middle, a steep drop to the inner coast; from the outer coast outward: a wide shallow shelf, then the slope to the trench. It is 0 at both coasts with a continuous slope through them. H(s) is the spine's height along the centreline, low at the tips and highest round the bend, and the sea deepens with it. Round the caps the cross-section goes polar, blending the two sides by angle. The sliders are the same θ and d as above, wherever they appear.

The cross-section is a smooth spline through a short table of control points, so it is a thing to edit rather than a formula to tune; the spine curve is a raised sine along the centreline. Both are smooth, so the erosion filter of a later step can take a gradient off them anywhere. In layers (5 sun each) the numbers are about 6.6 per metre.

Step 3: climate, ri by ri

Four numbers per ri from what the band already knows: where it is (northing for latitude, a for which coast, distance from the coast for continentality), how high it is, and which way it slopes. Temperature: a sea-level mean by latitude, 15.5 °C at the apex falling 0.036 °C per ri northward, minus 6.5 °C per 1,000 m; the coldest and warmest months sit a half-range either side of it, 10.5 °C at the coast widening to 13 °C well inland. Precipitation: 1,500 mm on the Pacific side blending to 2,200 on the Japan Sea side, plus 0.5 mm per metre of height, plus up to ±600 mm for slopes facing into or away from the winter NW monsoon and the summer SE rains.

Every term is a coefficient in the script, stylised from Honshu's stations: Tokyo 15.4 °C and 1,600 mm, Niigata 13.6 °C and 1,800 mm, Matsumoto 11.8 °C and 1,030 mm, Aomori 10.4 °C. The slope term needs a gradient, taken here by finite differences half a ri apart; the erosion filter later will want the same gradient, analytically. Sea ri are left blank.

Step 4: structure, fractal noise on the tectonic surface

Seeded gradient noise, summed over octaves, added to the step-2 height in metres everywhere, above and below the sea. The octaves are their own ladder: the coarsest spans the world, 36 ri, and each one halves, so every finer lattice nests in the coarser and a finer rung only adds octaves. The lattice is anchored to the origin, the world's centre, and turned so one axis runs along the arc's tangent there; that axis is stretched, so lumps come out as ridges with the tectonic grain. A seed's field leans one way across the arc; if its heavy side would fall seaward, the lattice is turned half a circle about the origin so the heavy side is inland. Octave k has wavelength 36 / 2ᵏ ri and amplitude A₀ · 2^(−kH): H is the Hurst exponent, and the coast this surface will have after erosion is a level set of dimension 2 − H. At the ri scale the five octaves down to 2¼ ri are evaluated; the table shows where the ladder goes below that.

The noise is gradient noise on a hex lattice: a seeded hash gives every node a unit gradient, a point takes the three nodes of its lattice triangle, and one kernel blends them, zero on the triangle's edges for every node but the edge's ends, so the blend is continuous and has no square-grid grain. The raw peak is exactly 1/√3, so √3 scales it to −1…1 and A₀ is the coarsest octave's swing in metres. Nothing here reads the coast: the shore, its land share, and the box-count against Kantō's 1.1–1.15 are read after erosion.

Step 5a: erosion, faded gullies on the step-4 surface

One octave of gullies, then more without feed-forward, so what 5b adds can be seen. At a point, octave k uses the step-4 lattice without the stretch, cells of 36 / 2ᵏ ri. The three nodes round the point are pivots; each pivot reads the downhill direction once, by central differences a quarter cell apart, and lays cos stripes across its slope, S per cell, and the same kernel blends the three into a gully value g from −1 in a crease to +1 on a ridge. The slope at the point sets a mask m: clamp σ / σ_ref, flip, square, flip, so it is 0 on the plain and rises gently. The target t that flat ground fades to comes from the surface's own relief at the octave's scale: how far the point stands above the mean of its four neighbours a quarter cell away, in units of half this octave's structure swing, clamped to ±1, so a plain gives 0 and stays put, a full ridge of this octave gives +1 and a crease −1. The octave adds E_k · (m·g + (1 − m)·t) metres, with E_k = E₀ · 2^(−kH) on the same ladder as the structure. What octave k reads for its slopes is the surface with structure octaves 0…k, the ones at least as wide as its cell, so a ri and a chō agree on every octave they share and a finer rung only appends.

Step 5b: erosion, each octave's slope fed to the next

The same octaves as 5a, but octave k + 1's pivots read the slope of the base plus the gullies octaves first to k already cut, and so does its mask. On the side of a coarse gully the slope points into it, so the finer stripes turn and run into it: that is the branching. A gully's slope is taken as the video's straight gullies, a triangle wave of the same amplitude and spacing, ±4·E·S/c across the slope by the sign of the sine, so fine gullies do not curl at the ends of the sine. Every pivot's direction is still a pure function of its node and seed, through the cache of coarser pivots, so a chō and a ri agree on every octave they share. The sliders are 5a's, linked.

Step 5c: erosion, stacked fading

5b, with each octave told what the octaves before it already made. One running value per point, R, is the normalised shape the octaves so far produced: −1 in a crease, +1 on a ridge, 0 to start. At octave k the target is R + (1 − |R|)·t_rel, so where the ground is already a coarse ridge or crease that wins and elsewhere the base's relief at this scale; the mask is the slope's times (1 − |R|)ᵖ, so no new gullies sit on top of an existing ridge or in an existing crease; the octave adds E_k·(m·g + (1 − m)·t) as before; and R becomes that shape, m·g + (1 − m)·t. At the first octave R is 0, so 5c is 5b plus the stacking, and p = 0 is 5b exactly. p is the video's terrain-detail knob. Everything is still per point, from values that are already pure functions of position and seed.

Step 5d: erosion, normalised gullies

5c, with the blend fixed. A pivot's stripes are a cos of the distance across its slope, and the kernel blends three pivots. Where the three are out of phase, a blend of cosines is a cosine with a smaller swing, so the gullies wash out there. Now each pivot's cos and sin are blended as a pair, a point on the unit circle; the blended pair's length is how much the pivots agree, from 1 down. Above the threshold the pair is divided back to length 1, so the gullies keep their full swing wherever the pivots agree at all; below it, where ridges and creases join and full normalising would make spikes, the pair is divided by the threshold instead, so the two join continuously and there is no step in the surface where the length crosses it. The straight-gully slope fed forward in 5b is unchanged. The threshold is one slider; off is 5c exactly.

Step 6: reading the coast

The coast is the zero set of the 5d surface; nothing here changes it, this step measures it. Land share against the inset: for every d from −10 to +30 ri, the world is placed there and its ri counted above sea level after step 2 and after step 4, which is cheap since neither has erosion; the share after 5d at the current d is marked, and since erosion costs a nearly constant few points, the d that gives 70% after erosion is read off the step-4 curve. The coast's dimension: a coast ri is a land ri with a sea neighbour; square boxes of 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 ri are laid over the world and the boxes holding coast counted, and the box-counting dimension D is minus the slope of log N against log s, fitted over 1 to 8 ri. The same is done at chō scale inside the picked window cluster, boxes of 1 to 32 chō, when the coast passes through it. Kantō's coast measures 1.11 in Natural Earth's 1:10m data and is likely 1.1 to 1.15 in truth; the structure noise alone would give level sets of dimension 2 − H.

Step n+1c: the rain settled, ri to ri

Also for later. Each ri's year of rain runs to its lowest neighbour, highest ri first, until it leaves the world's edge for a lower ri outside, in any of the six directions, or gets stuck in a pit. The sea inside the world is only low ground here; its water runs off the edge like any other. A pit fills to the height at which it would spill, so every pit is a lake with a capacity. Anything below 0 m is water already, the coast being the zero set, so a pit's fresh capacity runs from 0 m, or from its floor if that is higher, up to the spill: a basin the noise cut off from the sea becomes a lake whose fresh water sits above 0 m, and a pit whose spill is under the sea holds nothing. A lake keeps the year's inflow up to its capacity and passes the rest over its spill. The graph is the six faces of each ri, and the ring of ri outside the world is read from the same surface, so "off the map" is honest.
